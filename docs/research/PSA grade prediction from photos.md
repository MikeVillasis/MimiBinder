# Measure centering first, then estimate the grade

No publicly available method reliably predicts a Pokemon card's exact PSA grade from phone photos, and anyone who says otherwise is relying on unverified vendor numbers. One part of the problem is solvable in a free browser app: **centering**. It is the only PSA criterion with published numeric limits, and it can be computed deterministically with a perspective warp and sub-pixel edge fitting. The right first build is therefore a client-side centering tool. It reports front and back ratios and the highest PSA grade those ratios allow, rather than claiming to predict the grade. Corners and edges can be partly assessed with classical colour and contour analysis, most reliably on the blue back border. Surface (scratches, print lines, dents) is where single phone photos fail, because surface defects show up only under raking or multi-angle light. The best public learned model, a dual-branch ResNet with an ordinal loss trained on about 9.8k PSA-labelled pairs, reaches a quadratic weighted kappa of 0.84. That is good at ranking cards and says little about exact accuracy. PSA's own grades are noisy: resubmitted cards often move a full grade. Even a good model will probably land at about 50–70% exact and 85–95% within one grade, and those figures are an estimate from the notes, not a measurement. Vision LLMs are cheap enough to cost almost nothing at personal scale. They are poor at measuring pixel-level detail and tend to inflate grades, so they suit explaining a result or identifying the card, not choosing the score. The architecture below follows from that: deterministic centering in the browser, then classical corner and edge checks, then an optional LLM explanation layer behind a Vercel function, and a learned model only if you build your own labelled data.

## Only centering has published PSA numbers, which makes it the starting point

PSA grades on four factors: centering, corners, edges and surface. Only centering has published thresholds. The table below gives the widely quoted per-grade limits as of 2026, expressed as the larger border's share of the total border on each axis. PSA uses the worst reading, taken at the most off-centre part of the card ([CardCenteringCalculator](https://www.cardcenteringcalculator.com/articles/card-centering-tolerance-by-grade); [Rorz Cards](https://www.rorzcards.com/the-psa-grading-scale-explained-everything-you-need-to-know/)). In January 2025 PSA's site quietly changed the PSA 10 front standard to **"approximately 55/45"** and dropped the earlier 55/45–60/40 range. PSA's CEO described this as a wording change, not a change in practice ([Cardlines](https://cardlines.com/psa-centering-standards-change/)). Cards grading 7 or better also get a discretionary **5% front leeway** for eye appeal. A card that otherwise qualifies for a 9 can pass at 65/35 if it looks good ([Rorz Cards](https://www.rorzcards.com/the-psa-grading-scale-explained-everything-you-need-to-know/)). Half grades run from 1.5 to 8.5, and centering is the main factor that decides them. There is **no PSA 9.5**.

| PSA grade | Front max | Back max |
|---|---|---|
| 10 Gem Mint | ~55/45 | 75/25 |
| 9 Mint | ~60/40 | 90/10 |
| 8 NM-MT | ~65/35 | 90/10 |
| 7 NM | ~70/30 | 90/10 |
| 6 EX-MT | 80/20 | 90/10 |
| 5 EX / 4 VG-EX | 85/15 | 90/10 |
| 3 – 1.5 | 90/10 | 90/10 |

The other three factors are defined only in words, and those words translate into image features with very different difficulty. Corner "fraying" and "rounding" are geometric: deviation of the corner contour from the factory radius. Corner and edge "whitening" is a colour anomaly: bright, low-chroma pixels in a thin band just inside the cut edge. Edge "notching" and "chipping" show up as residuals from a fitted straight edge. Surface factors (gloss loss, scratches, print lines, dents, creases) depend on how light reflects off the card, and a single diffusely lit photo largely loses that information. PSA's qualifiers map the same way. Off-centre (OC) and miscut (MC) are geometric. Marks (MK) and staining (ST) are colour anomalies. Print defect (PD) and out-of-focus (OF) are hard to separate from blur in the photo itself ([Rorz Cards](https://www.rorzcards.com/the-psa-grading-scale-explained-everything-you-need-to-know/)). Two grade boundaries matter most to a collector. A **light crease caps a card at PSA 4**. The step from 9 to 10 is where Pokemon values jump, and it usually turns on the subtle surface and corner details that phone photos capture worst.

So no algorithm can derive corner, edge or surface thresholds from PSA's standards. They have to be calibrated against cards with known grades. Centering is the exception: it is a pure geometry problem with published targets.

## Deterministic centering works if the code measures the worst point, not the average

The measurement is simple: for each axis, the larger border divided by the sum of both borders, measured from the cut edge to the inner printed frame, on the front and the back. Engineering quality decides whether it is accurate. The best-documented method is the Kiradex app's scan-line border finder, reverse-engineered by [blakecrosley.com](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/how-card-centering-is-judged). It detects the card rectangle and pushes the corners out 5% so the true cut edge is captured. It warps the card to 800 px wide and casts 31 scan lines per side across the middle 30–70% of each side. A border is found where three consecutive pixels jump in RGB distance by more than 38, and the reported value is the median.

The same analysis shows why the median is a trap. A tilted (diamond-cut) frame read **50/50 by median, 54/46 by worst scan line and 60/40 at its true worst point**, because scan lines limited to the middle of each side never see the ends of the card. Resolution matters too. At about 0.087 mm/px after warping, a **1 px error moves the ratio by 0.9–1.8 points**, while the gap between 55/45 and 60/40 on a typical 2.4 mm modern border is only **0.24 mm** ([blakecrosley.com](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/how-card-centering-is-judged)).

A newer open-source engine, GroguZhang/card-grading-lab, shows the stronger design. It detects four points, fits ridge lines to the edges, warps to the 63.5×88.9 mm aspect ratio, and locates edges at sub-pixel precision weighted by gradient. It claims about **±0.2 percentage points** of typical error, and it includes PSA/BGS/CGC/SGC/TAG threshold tables plus draggable manual guide lines ([GitHub](https://github.com/GroguZhang/card-grading-lab)). The same teardown found that CardGrade.io's commercial browser centering does **no perspective correction at all**. It relies on Sobel plus row and column projection over a flat, straight-on shot, and has a red/blue channel swap bug in its grayscale conversion ([GitHub](https://github.com/GroguZhang/card-grading-lab)). Commercial does not mean rigorous.

The algorithm worth building, synthesised from these sources and standard OpenCV calls, works as follows.

First, find the card and its corners. Fit four lines to the outer edge, using RANSAC or Hough on edge points near each side, and take the corners where the lines intersect. Corners taken directly from the contour are pulled inward by Pokemon's rounded corners (about 3 mm radius).

Second, warp the card with a homography to a canonical canvas, for example 20 px/mm.

Third, find each edge at sub-pixel precision. At 50–100 positions along each side, outside the corner-radius zones, take an intensity profile perpendicular to the edge. Locate the edge at the peak of the derivative of the Gaussian-smoothed profile and refine it with 3-point parabolic interpolation.

Fourth, fit lines to both the outer cut edge and the inner frame edge. Border width then becomes a function of position along the side, so tilt is measured explicitly.

Finally, report the **worst** ratio on each axis, front and back, and map it to the highest PSA grade it allows, with an error band. The best guard against a known silent failure is a manual-correction overlay: when corner detection fails, some pipelines quietly default to 50/50. In one eBay photo dataset about 87% of raw attempts were discarded for this reason ([GitHub issue](https://github.com/Mango3356/pokemon-card-grader2/issues/1)).

Pokemon cards add specific traps. English cards switched from yellow to **silver borders with Scarlet & Violet (March 31, 2023)** ([PokeBeach](https://www.pokebeach.com/?p=265577)). Silver is low-chroma and close to white, so edges should be found in Lab or chroma gradients rather than grayscale, and whitening should be scored as an anomaly against the border's own statistics. Some sets are asymmetric by design. E-card sets have a deep left strip with a median left share of about 0.75. EX Dragon has a bottom border of about 5.1 mm against 1.5 mm at the top. From Sword & Shield on, small print sits in the bottom border, where a naive "first colour change" detector stops early ([blakecrosley.com](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/how-card-centering-is-judged)). Rules should therefore be keyed to the set, not the year.

Full-art fronts have no frame to measure. No grader publishes how it judges them, and guides disagree on whether graders use text boxes or a thin frame line ([blakecrosley.com](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/how-card-centering-is-judged); [cardgrade.io](https://cardgrade.io/blog/centering-guide)). The honest output for those is "front centering not measurable". The back's blue border is always measurable. An optional extension registers the photo against a well-centred catalogue scan of the same card. An independent check is available: the CC-BY GemSnipe dataset of **320 measured eBay photos of 302 Pokemon cards**, with PSA 10 and PSA 9 window pass flags. Its sample leans toward gem candidates, so it is good for sanity checks, not as ground truth ([GitHub](https://github.com/rrh1441/pokemon-card-centering-measurements)).

## Corners and edges partly survive a phone photo; surface mostly does not

The classical methods for the other factors are well understood, but none comes with validated thresholds for trading cards. Adaptive whitening detection samples the border's colour per image and scores white pixels against border-coloured pixels. The TCG-AI preprint describes this for blue Pokemon backs and combines HSV masks with a roundness measure for corner severity ([Research Square](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-8098898/v1.pdf)). CardGrade.io's engineering write-up describes 16 inspection zones (4 corners and 4 edges per face) scored by the **weakest zone**, with weakest-link caps. It says print lines are the main false positive for crease detection because "folds change the surface normal and ink does not." It calls a single phone photo "the weakest point for surface assessment" ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/cardgrade/building-a-photo-based-card-grader-computer-vision-where-the-llm-doesnt-pick-the-score-4461)). That team also found that upscaling small uploads to about 4000 px blurs out scratches. The fix was to compute geometry on the upscaled image and take defect crops from the original pixels.

Surface inspection depends on lighting more than on algorithms. Photometric stereo, which uses several shots under different light directions to recover surface normals, detects scratches on textured paper at **98.43% ± 1.0%** in a 2024 study ([DOAJ – Applied Sciences](https://doaj.org/article/e180751b3ae7486ebd024ed1be4e819a); [JIST](https://library.imaging.org/jist/articles/68/3/030503)). TAG Grading uses photometric stereo in its lab pipeline ([Phantom Display](https://phantomdisplay.com/blogs/blog/tag-card-grading-the-new-grader-explained)). A browser app can approximate this by asking for 2–4 extra shots with the phone flashlight held at a low angle from different sides. Glare from foil and sleeves can be reduced by registering several slightly angled frames and keeping a per-pixel soft minimum, the approach Google's PhotoScan uses for glossy prints ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/photoscan-taking-glare-free-pictures-of-pictures/)). Even so, no published work evaluates photometric stereo on glossy card stock or holofoil. Foil glitter will trigger scratch detectors unless foil areas are masked.

Phone capture limits the whole pipeline. Measuring centering to about 1% needs roughly **1,500–2,000+ px across the card**. A 12 MP still with the card filling most of the frame gives that; a 720p or 1080p video frame does not. That makes the browser camera API a real design constraint. `ImageCapture.takePhoto()` returns full sensor resolution but is Chromium-only (Chrome Android 157+) and **unsupported on iOS Safari through 27.2** ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/imagecapture); [Chrome Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/imagecapture?hl=en)). On iOS the reliable full-resolution path is `<input type="file" accept="image/*" capture="environment">`, which opens the native camera but gives up any live overlay ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/media-capturing-images)). An Apple forum report says repeated captures through that input overwrite each other, so each shot should be saved to IndexedDB immediately ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/826732)). The practical flow is hybrid. A live `getUserMedia` preview checks alignment, blur (Laplacian variance) and glare (ratio of saturated pixels). The final still is a full-resolution capture: `ImageCapture` on Android, the native camera hand-off on iOS. Cards should come out of sleeves and toploaders, be shot perpendicular from 25–40 cm with the 1x or 2x lens, and be lit with diffuse light ([Cardboard Connection](https://www.cardboardconnection.com/the-2026-collector-s-guide-to-diy-grading-best-phone-apps-ai-tools-to-stop-wasting-money-on-psa-submissions/amp)).

## Learned models top out at "good ranking", and every accuracy claim in the market is unverified

The peer-reviewed literature on deep learning for card grading is narrow. Nahar et al. classify **corner defects only**, reaching **83% mean accuracy** with DenseNet plus confidence calibration, up from 78% with VGG/Inception, on an industry dataset of undisclosed size ([LJMU Research Online](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/25294/)). The TCG-AI preprint combines YOLOv8 oriented bounding boxes for defects with a four-class DenseNet201 corner classifier and argues that CNNs beat classical OpenCV. Its numeric results could not be extracted ([Research Square](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-8098898/latest)).

Whole-card prediction exists only in hobby projects. The strongest documented one is **PSAGradePredictor**: a ResNet-18 front branch and a ResNet-34 back branch on 384 px LAB+CLAHE inputs, trained on **9,824 PSA-credited pairs**. With a CORAL ordinal loss it reaches **QWK 0.84 on validation**, up from 0.76 with weighted cross-entropy. It reports no exact accuracy, and its classes are heavily skewed (2,211 PSA 9s against 23 PSA 2s) ([GitHub](https://github.com/jshan9078/PSAGradePredictor)). An older VGG16 per-aspect project reports about 0.5 grades of average error ([GitHub](https://github.com/crimsonthinker/psa_pokemon_cards)). A baseball capstone claims 76% exact on a class-balanced set, which probably flatters real-world performance ([GitHub](https://github.com/BrianMillerS/trading_card_grading_capstone/blob/main/README.md)). One practitioner found that a single CNN did poorly. Specialist models per factor at different input resolutions worked better, and preprocessing mattered more than architecture ([DEV](https://dev.to/alex_newsome_c51e56ce7eb7/i-trained-an-ai-to-pre-grade-trading-cards-heres-what-i-learned-k5i)). For surface defects, industrial anomaly detectors such as PatchCore (up to **99.6% image AUROC on MVTec AD**) fit well, because a Pokemon card's clean reference artwork is available for comparison ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08265v1)). No one has published results applying them to cards.

The commercial landscape is crowded and largely unverifiable. Grading companies use machine vision internally. PSA bought machine-vision firm Genamint in 2021 and still describes its process as "human-led, technology-assisted" ([PrivSource](https://www.privsource.com/acquisitions/deal/professional-sports-authenticator-psa-acquires-genamint-ane_vXS2qp); [Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/psa-president-lays-plans-beckett-153000312.html)). TAG exposes a 1,000-point machine score but publishes no consistency data ([TAG](https://taggrading.com/pages/tagvsother)). PSA offers no consumer pre-grade tool.

The consumer apps contradict themselves. SnapGradeAI pages claim 87% within ±0.5 grades on 412 cards. Its own November 2026 roundup says it publishes no accuracy figure and did not run a blind test ([SnapGradeAI](https://www.snapgradeai.com/pokemon-card-grading); [SnapGradeAI roundup](https://www.snapgradeai.com/blog/best-ai-pokemon-card-grading-apps-2026)). PreGradeCards claims a 10,000-card benchmark at 89% within one grade, 68.4% exact and **82% on "PSA 10 or not"**, while admitting it built the tool "to win this comparison" and giving different figures on other pages ([PreGradeCards](https://pregradecards.com/blog/pregradecards-vs-cardgrade-snapgrade-cardsense-zeropop-2026)). The B2B Ximilar grading API, which many indie apps wrap, admits its surface grading "misses… by quite a large margin" in one example ([Ximilar](https://www.ximilar.com/ai-card-grading-automate-sports-cards-pre-grading)). No independent head-to-head test against actual PSA returns was found ([CardTracker AU](https://cardtracker.au/insights/guides/ai-pokemon-card-grading-apps)).

The ceiling comes from PSA itself. PSA publishes no consistency statistics. One collector resubmitted 25 cards and **only 3 came back with the same grade** ([Collectors forum](https://forums.collectors.com/discussion/comment/1264871/)), and one-grade swings on crack-and-resubmit are common. That sample is biased toward cards owners thought were undergraded. Even so, it means a single PSA label is a noisy target. Exact accuracy for any photo model probably peaks around 50–70% and within-one around 85–95%; the notes infer these figures and nobody has measured them. Within-one is also a weak bar for Pokemon, where the money is in the 9-versus-10 decision. A calibrated **P(PSA 10)** is the more useful output.

Data is the limiting resource. No large public dataset of graded Pokemon images exists. The options are a noisy 11.5k-image baseball set ([GitHub](https://github.com/samsilverman/PSA-Grades-Baseball)), Roboflow sets labelled for defects rather than grades ([Roboflow](https://universe.roboflow.com/rafets-workspace/card-grader-ogigp)), eBay graded listings fetched through your own API keys (the mint_condition route, about 90k cards) ([GitHub](https://github.com/rthorst/mint_condition/blob/master/README.md)), and PSA cert-page scans. Cert scans are slabbed cards under controlled light, a domain shift from raw cards on a desk ([PSA FAQ](https://www.psacard.com/faq)). PSA's terms could not be retrieved, so scraping carries unassessed terms-of-service risk.

Vision LLMs fail at exactly the measurements that matter. There is no rigorous benchmark of them on graded card photos. Anecdotal and vendor reports agree that they **inflate grades, returning 8–10 for cards PSA graded 6–8** ([PreGradeCards](https://pregradecards.com/blog/can-chatgpt-grade-cards-2026); [Chasing Majors](https://chasingmajors.substack.com/p/chatgpt-will-disrupt-the-sports-card)). This fits broader findings that VLMs do poorly on precise low-level visual tasks ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382111926_Vision_language_models_are_blind)). Claude's own documentation calls spatial outputs approximate and caps images at a 2,576 px long edge ([Claude Vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)). That is too coarse to judge a 0.24 mm centering margin.

## The browser carries the measurement and Vercel only relays requests

The deployment constraints point the same way as the accuracy evidence. Classical CV runs comfortably in the browser. Full OpenCV.js is about 8 MB of WebAssembly ([DEV](https://dev.to/awalariansyah/opencvjs-without-the-memory-leaks-chainable-image-processing-for-every-javascript-runtime-2e43)). Its threaded SIMD build is much faster but needs cross-origin isolation headers ([OpenCV docs](https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/d4/da1/tutorial_js_setup.html)). Scanic, a Rust/WASM document scanner, claims to be **under 100 KB gzipped** with about 10 ms warps; those are its own unbenchmarked figures ([Scanic README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/scanic@1.0.8/README.md)). Either works when cached by a service worker, although a sub-pixel edge fitter on the warped canvas is a few hundred lines of plain TypeScript and needs neither.

Small ONNX models (about 5–30 MB) run on ONNX Runtime Web through WASM ([ORT docs](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/tutorials/web/deploy.html)). WebGPU now ships in Safari 26 ([web.dev](https://web.dev/blog/webgpu-supported-major-browsers)), but an open WebKit bug reports failures on some iPhones, so WASM has to stay the fallback ([WebKit Bugzilla](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=301627)).

Vercel Hobby functions get 2 GB of memory, 1 vCPU and a 300 s limit, and **request bodies are capped at 4.5 MB**; the 20 MiB beta needs Pro. Time spent waiting on an external API does not count as active CPU ([Vercel limits](https://vercel.com/docs/functions/limitations)). A Hobby function is therefore a good thin proxy that keeps an LLM API key server-side. It is a poor place to host a model with heavy dependencies and slow cold starts. Images should be cropped to the detected card and downscaled on the client (about 1,500–2,500 px long edge, JPEG quality 0.85) so they stay well under the cap.

At personal scale, LLM cost does not matter. Using the notes' token arithmetic, a front-plus-back call costs about **$0.0013 on Claude Haiku 5.5, $0.026 on Sonnet 5.5 and $0.052 on Opus 5.5** ([Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). Gemini Flash-class models have a free tier, although Google uses free-tier inputs to improve its products ([Google AI Studio pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)). All three major providers support JSON-schema output.

### A four-phase build, ordered by evidence strength

| Phase | What it does | Where it runs | Expected quality | Main caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Deterministic centering | Guided capture, line-fit corners, homography, sub-pixel worst-point border ratios front and back, maps them to the highest PSA grade centering allows, manual drag-to-correct overlay | Browser only (TS + optional Scanic/OpenCV.js WASM) | Plausibly ±1–2 ratio points on well-shot bordered cards (unvalidated). Gives a reliable **grade ceiling** | Full-art fronts are unmeasurable. Silver borders and set-specific asymmetries need special handling. Says nothing about corners, edges or surface |
| 2. Classical corners and edges | 16-zone crops, Lab whitening scored against a sampled border reference (strongest on the blue back), corner-radius residuals, edge-line RANSAC residuals, glare and blur masks, weakest-link caps | Browser | Useful **red flags** (visible whitening, dings) that lower the ceiling. Thresholds must be tuned on your own graded cards | No published thresholds map to PSA grades. Silver fronts and foil cause false results |
| 3. LLM explanation layer (optional, on demand) | Sends defect crops plus the measured ratios to a Haiku/Flash-class model through a Vercel function. The model returns a JSON narrative and can identify the card and set, which selects set-specific border rules | Vercel Hobby proxy | Good descriptions and card identification. Grade opinions are **uncalibrated and biased upward** | The model must never set the centering number or the final grade. Each call costs fractions of a cent |
| 4. Learned surface and ordinal model (only with data) | Raking-light multi-shot capture, then reference-registered anomaly detection (PatchCore-style) and a small ordinal (CORAL) head over the phase 1–2 features, giving a P(PSA 10) and a grade distribution | Browser (ONNX Runtime Web, WASM) | If trained on a few thousand of your own photo/grade pairs: perhaps 50–70% exact and 85–95% within one at best, capped by PSA's own inconsistency | Requires a labelled dataset that does not exist publicly. Cert scans differ from raw phone photos |

The phases are ordered by how much evidence supports each one. Phase 1 is fully deterministic and can be checked against a ruler. It should be validated with about 10 cards scanned at 1200 dpi as ground truth, photographed 5 times each. Ratios should be shown to whole numbers only if the worst error stays at or under about 1 point ([blakecrosley.com](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/how-card-centering-is-judged)). Phase 2 should be presented as flags with evidence crops, not subgrades. A "probable ceiling: PSA 8 (back corner whitening)" message is honest; a "Corners 8.5" subgrade is not. Phase 3 is cheap and pleasant, but only if the prompt passes the measured numbers in as facts and asks for explanation, not judgement. Phase 4 becomes worthwhile only once the app has stored your own pre-submission photos alongside the grades PSA later returned. That logged dataset is the asset no public source offers, and in a personal app it builds up naturally.

## Conclusion

The useful reframing is that a photo-based tool can reliably prove what a card *cannot* get, not what it *will* get. Centering, measured at the worst point and compared with PSA's published limits, gives a hard ceiling. Visible whitening and dings lower that ceiling further. Everything that separates a 9 from a 10 on a well-centred modern card lies mostly beyond what a phone photo can resolve: hairline scratches, print lines, faint dents, and the 5% eye-appeal leeway. So does PSA's own grade-to-grade inconsistency. A tool that says "centering 57/43 front, 68/32 back; best possible PSA 9; no visible corner or edge damage; surface not assessed" is more truthful, and in practice more valuable, than one that prints a confident "PSA 9.5". PSA does not even issue a 9.5.

The second takeaway is that the competitive edge in this space is data, not models. Every vendor accuracy figure found is self-reported and internally inconsistent, and the best public model is a hobby project. A personal app that logs predictions before submission and records the grade PSA returns will, after a few dozen submissions, have the only honest accuracy measurement its owner can trust. It will also have the training data a phase-4 model needs.
